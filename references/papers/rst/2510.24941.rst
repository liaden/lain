.. rubric:: Abstract

=-1

Large language models can generate long chain-of-thought (CoT) reasoning, yet prior work suggests that CoT can be post-hoc rationalization rather than a faithful reflection of the computation through explicitly designed settings. In this work, we go further and propose a True Thinking Score (TTS) to quantify the causal contribution of each step in CoT to the model's final prediction in realistic reasoning problems. Across eleven models ranging from 1.5B to 1.1T parameters on common reasoning benchmarks, we find that CoTs often interleave *true-thinking* steps, which causally affect the final answer, with *decorative-thinking* steps, which appear useful but have little causal influence; Such decorative steps remain prevalent even for frontier models: Over 30% of steps in Kimi-K2.6 are decorative on MATH with TTS ≤ 0.005. Furthermore, TTS enables effective CoT pruning: removing 50% of CoT steps with the lowest TTS can largely maintain the performance. Self-training on these pruned CoTs reduces reasoning length by 66% while preserving performance on Nemotron3-Nano-30B. Finally, we provide a mechanistic analysis showing that LLMs can be steered in the latent space to engage or disengage with reasoning steps. Overall, our results reveal that frontier LLMs often verbalize reasoning steps that are not causally used, challenging both the efficiency and the trustworthiness of CoT [].

Introduction
------------

=-100
Recent frontier LLMs can increasingly solve complex reasoning problems through test-time scaling, often by generating a very long chain of thought (CoT) [guo2025deepseek]_ [muennighoff2025s1]_ [snell2024scaling]_ [jaech2024openai]_. In their long CoT, these models frequently exhibit an “aha moment”, where the model begins to *self-verify* its solution [guo2025deepseek]_. LLMs' generated CoT is commonly assumed as a scratch pad where the model thinks out loud [korbak2025chain]_. This has also motivated using CoT as a means to monitor LLMs and detect unsafe behaviors revealed in their CoT [baker2025monitoring]_.

=-100
A central assumption behind CoT is faithfulness: the verbalized reasoning should reflect the computation that produces the model's final answer. Prior work shows that this assumption can fail. Models may rely on inserted hints [chen2025reasoning]_ [chua2025deepseek]_ [turpin2023language]_ or spurious biases [arcuschin2025chain]_ without acknowledging them in their CoT, and may commit to an answer before completing the reasoning trace [ma2025reasoning]_ [wang-etal-2025-chain]_ [yang2025dynamic]_. These findings suggest that CoT steps can be post-hoc rationalizations rather than causally used parts of the prediction process.

However, prior work largely relies on controlled settings and remains suggestive, typically showing CoT can be unfaithful at the level of the entire reasoning trace. It still remains unclear how each individual CoT step truly contributes to the model's final prediction in common reasoning tasks. This gap limits the use of CoT as a monitoring signal. If a reasoning step does not causally affect the prediction, inspecting it may give a misleading view of the model's computation, especially in high-stakes settings [zhong2025impossiblebench]_ [baker2025monitoring]_.

=-1000
To close this gap, we propose the True-Thinking Score (TTS), a step-wise causal analysis of CoT that quantifies how much each verbalized step affects the model’s prediction. We define **true-thinking** steps as those that causally affect the prediction, and **decorative-thinking** steps as those that make minimal causal contribution to the prediction, as illustrated in Figure unfaithful-self-check. is adapted from Average Treatment Effect (ATE) [rubin1974]_ [pearl2009causality]_ with two complementary interventions by conditioning on its context steps to evaluate its necessity and sufficiency to the model's prediction (Section score). On common reasoning benchmarks, we evaluate eight frontier models across different scales and find that decorative steps remain widespread (Section eval). On MATH benchmark, nearly 60% of Qwen3.6-35B's CoT steps have ≤ 0.005, indicating minimal causal impact on the final answer. Larger models tend to achieve higher , but still contain many decorative steps: for Nemotron3-Super-120B, nearly 30% of steps are decorative. Moreover, true-thinking and decorative-thinking steps are often interleaved within the same CoT, raising concerns about using CoT as a monitoring signal for model misbehavior [baker2025monitoring]_.

=-1000
We next show that enables effective CoT pruning (Section prune-cot). Removing the 50% lowest- steps largely preserves performance, while removing high- steps sharply degrades reasoning accuracy. TTS-based pruning also outperforms an expert prompt-compression LM and direct prompting for concise reasoning. Further self-training on pruned CoTs reduces reasoning length by 66% while preserving performance, and generalizes to held-out dataset.

=-1000
Finally, we provide a mechanistic study of step-level causal use (Section exp-steer). We demonstrate that steering the hidden states of a reasoning step can mediate whether the model uses that step to compute the answer. The steering direction is extracted from the contrast between low- and high- steps on one dataset, yet generalizes to other datasets and appears to modulate the model's attention to the targeted step. In particular, steering along the direction can induce the model to reason over otherwise decorative self-verification steps and correct previous errors.

Overall, our core contributions are threefold.
- * We propose to quantify the causal contribution of each step in a CoT.
  We reveal that decorative thinking steps—steps with minimal causal contribution—are prevalent in frontier models on common reasoning benchmarks.

    * We show that  can be used to prune CoTs without degrading performance, and that further self-training on pruned CoTs improves reasoning efficiency.

    * We provide a mechanistic study, revealing for the first time that whether the model uses a reasoning step to compute the answer can be mediated through steering in the latent space.

Together, these findings call for more future work on improving the causality of LLM reasoning, making CoTs more trustworthy and efficient for real-world deployment.

Measuring Step-wise Causality in Reasoning
------------------------------------------

=-1000

In this section, we adapt the Average Treatment Effect (ATE) [rubin1974]_ [pearl2009causality]_ to measure the causal impact of individual steps \( s \) on LLMs' prediction result \( y^* \). We define that a step with genuine causal impact is a **true-thinking step**, where the model follows \(s\) in order to produce \(y^*\). By contrast, a step with minimal causal impact on \(y^*\) is a **decorative-thinking step**. We first detail the notations and explain the True-Thinking Score in Section score.

Given a model's predicted answer \(y^*\), we measure the *causal contribution* of each reasoning step \(s\).
A step with genuine causal impact is a **true thinking step**, meaning the model actually reasons over \(s\) to produce \(y^*\).
A step with no causal impact is a **decorative thinking step**, where the model verbalizes reasoning without using it in computation, serving only as a gesture of reasoning or post-hoc rationalization.

**Notation and Setup.** We adopt notation following [rubin1974]_ [pearl2009causality]_.
Let the input question be \( q \), and let the model’s full chain of thought (CoT) for \( q \) be
\(
\mathcal{C}^{⋆} = (s_1, s_2, …, s_n),
\)
where each \( s_i \) denotes a reasoning step.
At the current step \( s\) under evaluation (we omit the index i and directly use s for simplicity), we define the **context** as its preceding steps, i.e.,
\(
\mathcal{C} = (s_1, s_2, …, s_{i-1})
\).
To probe the model’s current prediction after any partial reasoning trace, we use early-exit answering by appending a standardized cue: The final result is. This approach, following [lanham2023measuring]_ [fu2025deep]_ [yang2025dynamic]_ [tanneru2024hardness]_ [bogdan2025thought]_, reliably elicits the model’s intermediate answer given the question \( q \) and reasoning prefix \((\mathcal{C}, s)\).
Let \( f(q, \mathcal{C}, s) \) denote the model’s early-exit prediction after processing \( q \) with context \(\mathcal{C}\) and step \( s \).
The *reference prediction* under the full reasoning trace is then defined as
\(
y^{*} := f(q, \mathcal{C}^{⋆}),
\)
representing the model’s final answer when all steps in the full CoT are intact.

**Indicator Variables.** We introduce the following binary random variables to formalize interventions on reasoning steps: (1) **Context indicator** \(\mathbf{C} ∈ \{0,1\}\):
  \(\mathbf{C}{=}1\) indicates an intact context (the original prefix \(\mathcal{C})\);
  \(\mathbf{C}{=}0\) indicates a perturbed context in which all preceding steps are replaced by perturbed versions.
  We write \( c ∈ \{0,1\} \) for a specific realization of \(\mathbf{C}\).  (2) **Step toggle** \(\mathbf{X} ∈ \{0,1\}\):
  \(\mathbf{X}{=}1\) is the original step \( s \);
  \(\mathbf{X}{=}0\) replaces it with a perturbed version \( s' \). (3) **Outcome indicator** \(\mathbf{Y} ∈ \{0,1\}\):
  Given \( f(q, \mathcal{C}, s) \), we define
  \(
  \mathbf{Y} := \mathbf{1} \{ f(q, \mathcal{C}, s) = y^{*} \},
  \)
  which measures whether the model’s early-exit prediction under the given intervention matches the full-CoT reference outcome.

**Perturbation Procedure.**
To isolate the causal effect of each reasoning step, we create perturbed versions of steps and contexts by introducing *small random numerical offsets* to quantities appearing in the reasoning text [lanham2023measuring]_ [gaoshapley]_.
These perturbations are minimal and preserve grammatical and semantic structure, ensuring that the modified step remains coherent but subtly altered.
Additional implementation details are provided in Appendix imp.

Context-based Average Treatment Effect
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

The Average Treatment Effect (ATE) [rubin1974]_ [pearl2009causality]_ quantifies the causal effect of a binary intervention \mathbfX∈0,1 on an outcome \mathbfY via Pearl's \mathrmdo(·) operator:

.. math::

   ATE  =  S_1 - S_0,

where \mathrmS_x∈0,1 := \Pr\!\big(\mathbfY=1 \big| \mathrmdo(\mathbfX=x)\big). To evaluate the causal contribution of a reasoning step s for LLMs' reasoning, we condition on a *context setting* \mathbfC∈0,1 and define a *context-based ATE*:

.. math::

   ATE(c)     =  S_1(c) - S_0(c),

   S_x∈{0,1}(c)     :=  (𝐘=1  |  𝐂=c,  do(𝐗=x)),

where c∈0,1 specifies the context regime and \mathbfX toggles using the intact step s versus its perturbed counterpart s'. We consider two regimes:
a) *Intact context* (\mathbfC=1): the original prefix \mathcalC preceding s is kept as generated;
b) *Perturbed context* (\mathbfC=0): all steps in \mathcalC are minimally perturbed (e.g., by small numeric offsets), weakening associations between s and other steps so that the effect of s can be isolated.

**Scoring the outcome.** Let y^* := f(q,\mathcalC^(⋆)) be the model’s reference answer obtained via *early-exit* on the full CoT, and let f(q,\mathcalC,s) denote the early-exit prediction after a given (\mathcalC,s).
Instead of the binary \mathbfY=\mathbf1f(·)=y^*, we use the model’s confidence for the event y^*: \Pr(\mathbfY=1 \mid ·) \equiv \Pr\big(y^* \mid q,\mathcalC,s\big), giving \mathrmS_x(c) a probabilistic (confidence-based) interpretation.

""""

Interpreting \mathrmATE_\textnec(1) vs. \mathrmATE_\textsuf(0). Conditioning on \mathbfC allows us to distinguish two complementary notions of causal relevance:

- * **Necessity under intact context (\mathrmATE_\textnec(1)).**

  .. math::

     ATE_nec(1) = (y^* |𝐂=1, do(𝐗=1))

     - (y^* |𝐂=1, do(𝐗=0)).

  =-1
      This tests whether s is *needed* given the full, supportive context \mathcalC.
      A low \mathrmATE_\textnec(1) indicates that removing s does not harm performance when other steps remain intact.

    * **Sufficiency under perturbed context (\mathrmATE_\textsuf(0)).**

  .. math::

     ATE_suf(0)
         = (y^* |𝐂=0, do(𝐗=1))

         - (y^* |𝐂=0, do(𝐗=0)).

      This asks whether s can *on its own*—i.e., with weakened support from \mathcalC—drive the model toward y^*.
      A high \mathrmATE_\textsuf(0) suggests s is **sufficiently informative** to elicit the correct answer, capturing causal relevance even when s is not strictly necessary under the intact context.

=-100
Together, \mathrmATE_\textnec(1) (necessity) and \mathrmATE_\textsuf(0) (sufficiency) provide a balanced view: A step can be causally meaningful by being necessary, sufficient, or both.

""""

True-Thinking Score (TTS). Finally, the score is defined as

.. math::

   (s)
   = 12(|ATE_nec(1)| + | ATE_suf(0)|).

A smaller s indicates that the step has little causal influence on the model's prediction: perturbing or keeping it leads to almost the same result. Thus, that step is more likely to be *decorative* rather than *true thinking*. For each context setting \(c\), we measure the unsigned \mathrmATE(c),
\(|\mathrm{ATE}(c)| = |S_1(c)-S_0(c)|\). The sign of \(\mathrm{ATE}(c)\) reflects whether the step is helpful or harmful (e.g., the step is actually wrong) overall, but we are interested in *how much* the model truly thinks through the step in its internal computation, regardless of direction. Taking the absolute value thus captures the magnitude of a step's causal effect and provides a broader measure of its importance. is a continuous metric, where lower values indicate that a step is more decorative.

Mediating Reasoning via Steering
--------------------------------

In this section, we further provide mechanism insights on decorative thinking in CoT through steering. We empirically reveal that, in LLMs, latent-space steering with the direction identified by can mediate whether to use a reasoning step in CoT when computing the results. Steering the hidden states of a step along direction can force LLMs to engage with that step to compute its answer and may increase the attention to that step as well and vice versa.

""""

Extracting the steering direction with .
We first detail how to extract the steering direction. We focus on the residual stream activation h^(l)(s_t) of the last token position t for a step s at a layer l. At a layer l, we collect the hidden states of the most representative steps s_\textTT (where s_TT ≥ threshold α) and steps s_\textDT (where s_DT ≤ threshold β). Following the difference-in-means approach [marks2023geometry]_ [arditi2024refusal]_ [zhao2025llms]_, we compute the direction as the mean shift from μ^(l)_\textTT=\textmean(h^(l)(s_\textTT)) to μ^(l)_\textDT=\textmean(h^(l)(s_\textDT)) in the latent space, i.e., v^l_\direction = μ^l_\mathrmTT - μ^l_\mathrmDT. For steering at test time, we modify the residual stream for the hidden state of a test step in the example by using activation addition at a single layer l, i.e., \barh^(l)=h^(l)+v^(l)_\direction to all tokens in the step. Detailed implementations are in Appendix steering-imp.

Specifically, we have two steering cases, **(1) Engagement Test**: We consider cases where f(q,\mathcalC)=y_GT and f(q,\mathcalC,s')=y_GT. Namely, the model still obtains the ground-truth answer y_GT despite the perturbed step s'. If we apply a steering direction v_^l to the hidden state of s', and the model's correct answer flips to an incorrect one (f^+v_^l(q,\mathcalC,s')≠ y_GT), this indicates that the steering has forced the model to reason over s', thus following the errors injected into s'. **(2) Disengagement Test**: On the other hand, we consider the reverse cases where the model first predicts the correct answer, i.e., f(q,\mathcalC)=y_GT, but including a perturbed step s' causes it to fail: f(q,\mathcalC,s')≠ y_GT. If applying a steering direction -v_^l to s' flips the wrong answer to the correct answer (f^-v_^l(q,\mathcalC,s')=y_GT), then the intervention has made the model disregard the step s'.

=-100
As comparison baselines, we consider three approaches for layer-wise intervention apart from the direction. (1) DropStep direction: Motivated by the prior work [tanneru2024hardness]_ [lanham2023measuring]_ [bogdan2025thought]_ [wang-etal-2025-chain]_ on faithfulness evaluation, we compare prediction confidence before and after dropping step s, i.e., \mathrmP(y_GT|q,\mathcalC,o)-\mathrmP(y_GT|q,\mathcalC) (equivalent to \mathrmATE_\textnec(1) in our framework). The steps of the highest and lowest scores are then used to extract a steering direction following the same method as the direction. (2) Random steering vector: we generate a random vector with the same dimensionality and norm as the direction to test whether our identified direction encodes meaningful information. (3) Attention scaling: we scale the attention weights of the tokens of step s at a layer during inference time, with scale=100 encouraging the model to think through the step and scale=0 suppressing it.

In the , it effectively prevents the model from using the perturbed step s', with effects far stronger than those of random vectors. On the other hand, our experiments across datasets show that the latent signal controlling whether a step engages in reasoning is universal. As seen in Table intervention-results, the direction extracted on AMC generalizes well to other datasets across all models, indicating a model-internal mechanism of thinking rather than a dataset-specific artifact. In the Qwen-2.5 model, layer 15-22 consistently yield the strongest intervention performance across all three datasets (Figure steer-c–steer-d), suggesting these intermediate layers may concentrate latent reasoning. Related Work
------------

=-1000

""""

Internal thinking in LLMs' latent space. Apart from explicit CoT, LLMs “think” internally across their layers. They can directly answer reasoning problems, sometimes even matching the performance of CoT-based prompting [ma2025reasoning]_. Prior work suggests that LLMs may solve certain tasks through internal *circuits* [yang2024large]_ [marks2024sparse]_ [prakash2025language]_. The *implicit* reasoning capabilities of LLMs may bypass using explicit CoTs [deng2023implicitcot]_ [hao2024coconut]_ [pfau2024let]_ [goyal2023think]_. Such internal reasoning of LLMs questions how much the model truly uses each step in their CoT, which we study using a causal framework.

=-1000

""""

The unfaithfulness of LLMs' reasoning traces. Prior work has raised broad concerns about the faithfulness of reasoning traces. Models may rely on hidden hints or spurious biases without acknowledging them in CoT [chen2025reasoning]_ [arcuschin2025chain]_ [chua2025deepseek]_ [turpin2023language]_, and generated rationales may be insensitive to perturbations or unnecessary for reaching the final answer [gaoshapley]_ [lanham2023measuring]_ [tanneru2024hardness]_ [wang-etal-2025-chain]_. While these studies provide important evidence that CoT can be unfaithful or post-hoc, they mostly evaluate CoT holistically, leaving the causal role of individual steps underexplored.

Steering LLMs. Steering directions applied to intermediate hidden states have been found to mediate LLMs' behaviors or perception in many aspects [von2024language]_ [turner2023steering]_ [tigges2023linear]_ [li2023inference]_ [marks2023geometry]_ [zhao2025llms]_. In terms of reasoning, past works have found steering vectors that can control the strength of reasoning, e.g., longer or shorter CoT [tang2025glore]_ [sun2025thinkedit]_ [chen2025seal]_ [sheng2025reasoningstrength]_ or different reasoning styles in CoT [venhoff2025understanding]_.

Conclusions
-----------

=-1000
We propose True-Thinking Score to analyze step-wise causality in CoT and show that frontier LLMs contain many decorative steps that make minimal causal contribution to the model's answer on common reasoning benchmarks. We reveal that *true-thinking* and *decorative-thinking* steps are often interleaved: only a small subset causally influences the final prediction, while many steps merely create the appearance of reasoning. We further show that can prune CoTs into concise rationales for self-training, substantially improving reasoning efficiency without sacrificing performance. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that whether a reasoning step contributes to the model's computation can be mediated by a direction, suggesting that step-level use is reflected in internal representations. Broadly, our findings raise concerns about both the efficiency of CoT reasoning of current LLMs and the reliability of using CoT to interpret LLMs' internal thought.

Limitations
-----------

Our causal evaluation framework is inherently approximate and especially focuses on math reasoning that has strong logic. It is greedy in nature and may not capture all possible causal pathways, nor does it aim to reconstruct a complete causal graph of reasoning steps. Thus, it should be viewed as a probe that highlights representative *true-thinking* and *decorative-thinking* steps rather than a definitive oracle of internal reasoning. However, we can still leverage to prune CoT effectively. In addition, the direction we extract may not be optimal. We regard our findings as an existence proof that whether to engage with a reasoning step can be mediated by steering directions, and we leave the development of more effective directions and a deeper understanding of their geometry to future work.

Taxonomy of Low-TTS Reasoning Steps
-----------------------------------

To better understand which reasoning steps receive low , we categorize low-TTS steps into a small set of functional types. This taxonomy is intended as an interpretability tool rather than a learned classifier. Each step is assigned to exactly one category using a priority-ordered rule set based on lexical and structural cues, so that broad classes such as calculation do not absorb more specific functions such as verification or restatement.

""""

Verification.
Steps that explicitly check or validate a previous result, with cues such as “check,” “verify,” “substitute,” or “sanity check.”

""""

Restatement.
Steps that restate an intermediate result/ condition or summarize a derivation, often with cues such as “therefore,” “thus”.

""""

Calculation.
Steps that contain explicit numerical or algebraic manipulation, identified by mathematical operators, equations, fractions, or digits. This class is particularly important: low-TTS calculations show that even symbolic-looking computation can be causally inert. In many cases, perturbing numbers in these steps leaves the final answer unchanged, suggesting that the calculation is redundant or post-hoc.

""""

Setup.
Steps that introduce notation, variables, or representations, with cues such as “let,” “define,” “denote,” “set,” or “represent.” Low-TTS setup steps often correspond to conventional problem translation: they may make the solution easier to read, but the model may not rely on them to compute the answer.

""""

Alternative route.
Steps that introduce an optional solution path, with cues such as “alternative,” “another way,” or “could also.”

""""

Other.
Steps that do not match the above patterns are assigned to an “other” category. This residual bucket captures heterogeneous cases and is not interpreted as a coherent reasoning type.

More related work

""""

Detailed analysis of existing evaluation frameworks of faithfulness for CoT. Many recent works have sought to evaluate the faithfulness of reasoning traces. Most, however, focus on the CoT as a whole, providing suggestive evidence that the CoT is not faithful without analyzing each step. The existing evaluation methods can be summarized as,

- * **Hint-based evaluation:** Most prior studies [chen2025reasoning]_ [arcuschin2025chain]_ [chua2025deepseek]_ [turpin2023language]_ consider simple multiple-choice questions rather than complex reasoning tasks. Hints are injected into questions that the model initially failed to solve. A faithful CoT should explicitly acknowledge the use of hints in deriving the correct answer [chua2025deepseek]_ [chen2025reasoning]_. Relatedly, [arcuschin2025chain]_ [turpin2023language]_ insert biasing features into questions and observe whether the model's answer changes. If so, the CoT is deemed unfaithful, as the prediction is driven by bias in the prompt. Yet, those framework setups are not generalizable to practical reasoning problems, and cannot reveal the faithfulness of individual steps.

    * **Perturbation-based evaluation:** Errors are injected into a correct reasoning step, and its following reasoning traces are resampled [gaoshapley]_ [lanham2023measuring]_. If the model's predicted answer remains unchanged at last, the CoT is considered unfaithful since the error inserted was ignored. However, this criterion may be unreliable: the model may instead detect and correct the error in later resampled steps. [yee2024dissociation]_ [xiong2025measuring]_ try to address this by tracking resampled steps.

    * **Early-exit answering:** Early-exit cues are inserted after a reasoning step to test whether the model can already produce a correct answer [lanham2023measuring]_ [tanneru2024hardness]_. A correct early-exit answer suggests the CoT may be unfaithful [lanham2023measuring]_ since further steps are unnecessary to model's answer. [tanneru2024hardness]_ further computes the change in the model's answer confidence before and after each reasoning step when using early-exit answering. Yet unnecessity may not be equivalent to unfaithfulness.  The fact that a model arrives at the correct answer early and maintains it does not necessarily imply that it ignores subsequent reasoning steps. This view overlooks important cases where the model continues to engage in those steps, for example, faithfully performing self-verification to consolidate or reinforce earlier predictions.

 On the other hand, conceptually, CoTs have also been hypothesized as either *CoT-as-computation* or *CoT-as-rationalization* [emmons2025chain]_. However, our analysis in Section eval reveals a more nuanced picture: reasoning traces in CoT often interleave steps that genuinely drive computation with others that merely pretend reasoning.

""""

CoT pruning. Our work is also related to CoT compression [xia2025tokenskip]_ [song2025reasoning]_ [kang2025c3ot]_ [yu2025causal]_ and important steps analysis [golovneva2022roscoe]_. However, those works are different as they focus on the semantics of verbalized steps for analysis and do not look into the underlying causality. For example, [xia2025tokenskip]_ prune long CoTs into shorter sentences with equivalent meanings for LLMs without degrading performance. But that does not mean pruned steps do not causally contribute to the model's answer. Our work may suggest similar results that there exist shorter alternative CoTs leading the model to get the same answers, but they do not solve whether a step truly engages with the model's computation, and the internal mechanism of LLMs' thinking process is still unclear. [yu2025causal]_ reframe CoT pruning into the Probability of Necessity and Sufficiency (PNS) framework [pearl2009causality]_, but it focuses on estimating the necessity of each step using counterfactual intervention in a similar way to prior works [lanham2023measuring]_ [yee2024dissociation]_ [bogdan2025thought]_. Although they are framed into PNS, their measurement of sufficiency and necessity is restricted to contexts where the prior steps remain unchanged, which corresponds to \mathrmATE_\textnec(1) in our framework.

Implementations
---------------

""""

Perturbing reasoning steps. We treat sentences as distinct reasoning steps, as prior work has shown that each sentence can serve a different function within a reasoning trace [bogdan2025thought]_. We follow prior work [lanham2023measuring]_ [gaoshapley]_ and add small random offsets (chosen from [-3,-2,-1,1,2,3]) to the numbers in a reasoning step. This keeps the perturbation minimal so that the step remains largely unchanged in token length, wording, and underlying logic. We can therefore reasonably attribute any confidence changes caused by the perturbation to the model’s treatment of the original step. For steps that do not contain numerical values, we also follow prior work [bogdan2025thought]_ [tanneru2024hardness]_ [wang-etal-2025-chain]_ by dropping them as a perturbation to measure the influence of those sentences. For perturbing context steps, we only change numerical values.

When extracting steering directions with difference-in-means, the steps with zero are treated as decorative-thinking steps (s_\textDT), while we use steps from different ranges of as true-thinking steps (s_\textTT). Because the directions are computed as the difference in mean hidden states between true and decorative steps (Eq. dir), negligible steering effects mean the direction fails to capture the meaningful distinction between decorative and true thinking.
